E-numbers: what food additive codes mean and which to watch

Vertex One · Updated 2026-11-18

Many people get worried when they see “E” and a number in the ingredients. In fact an E-number simply shows that the additive has been assessed and approved in the European Union. Some are ordinary substances such as vitamin C. But a few deserve attention from certain people.

What the numbers mean

  • E100–E199 — colours
  • E200–E299 — preservatives
  • E300–E399 — antioxidants and acidity regulators
  • E400–E499 — thickeners, stabilisers, emulsifiers
  • E600–E699 — flavour enhancers
  • E900 and up — glazing agents, sweeteners and others

“E” doesn't always mean artificial

E300 is ascorbic acid (vitamin C), E330 citric acid, E160a beta-carotene and E440 pectin from apples and citrus. Additives are allowed only in set amounts, and EFSA re-evaluates their safety from time to time.

What is really worth watching

  • Six colours — E102, E104, E110, E122, E124, E129. In the EU, foods with them must carry a warning that they may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children.
  • Sulphites (E220–E228) — in wine, dried fruit and some sauces. They can cause reactions in sensitive people, including some with asthma, and must be highlighted as an allergen.
  • Aspartame (E951) — products must state that they contain a source of phenylalanine, which matters for people with phenylketonuria.
  • Nitrites (E249–E250) — in sausages and cured meats. Not dangerous within permitted amounts, but processed meat in general is worth limiting.
  • Titanium dioxide (E171) — no longer authorised in food in the EU since 2022, after EFSA's re-evaluation.
It's more useful to look at the whole ingredient list than at single codes: the shorter the list and the closer the food is to its natural form, the better. A long list of additives is a sign of a highly processed product.

How to read the ingredients

  1. Ingredients are listed in descending order of weight: the first one is the largest.
  2. An additive may appear as a code (E330) or a name (citric acid) — they're the same thing.
  3. Allergens are shown in bold or another colour.

If you have an allergy, an intolerance or a child with dietary restrictions, always check the original label and ask your doctor.
