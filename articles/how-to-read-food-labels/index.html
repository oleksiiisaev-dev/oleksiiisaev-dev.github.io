How to read a food label: ingredients, allergens, sugar and salt

Vertex One · Updated 2026-11-18

A label tells you more about a product than the advertising on the pack. In a minute you can see what it's made of, how much sugar and salt it has and whether it's safe for people with allergies.

1. Ingredients: order matters

Ingredients are listed in descending order of weight. If sugar is first or second, there's a lot of it. The percentage of the main ingredient is often given too, such as “strawberries 12%” in a yoghurt.

2. Allergens

In the EU, 14 major allergens must be highlighted in the ingredients (in bold or colour): cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soy, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphites, lupin and molluscs.

“May contain traces of…” means the product was made near an allergen. That matters for people with severe allergies.

3. Nutrition per 100 g

The table shows energy, fat (including saturates), carbohydrate (including sugars), protein and salt. Compare products per 100 g, not per serving — manufacturers' servings differ.

  • Sugars above 22.5 g per 100 g is high; 5 g or less is low.
  • Salt above 1.5 g per 100 g is high; 0.3 g or less is low.
  • Saturated fat above 5 g per 100 g is high; 1.5 g or less is low.

These thresholds come from the UK “traffic light” labelling scheme and are handy for a quick comparison.

4. Sugar under other names

Glucose or glucose-fructose syrup, dextrose, maltose, invert sugar, honey and fruit juice concentrate are all added sugars. WHO advises keeping free sugars below 10% of daily energy, ideally below 5%.

A quick check in the shop: look at the first three ingredients, the “sugars” and “salt” lines per 100 g and the highlighted allergens. That's enough to choose the better of two similar products.

5. Dates

“Use by” is about safety: don't eat the food after that date. “Best before” is about quality: after the date the food may still be safe, but taste or texture may be worse.
