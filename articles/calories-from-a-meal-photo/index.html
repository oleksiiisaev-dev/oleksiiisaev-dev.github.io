How to count calories from a meal photo: accuracy and tips

Vertex One · Updated 2026-11-18

Weighing every bite is reliable, but few people keep it up for long. A meal photo with a calorie estimate is a faster way to keep a food diary. It helps to remember that it is an estimate and to know how to make it better.

How it works

AI recognises the dish and its ingredients in the photo, estimates the portion in grams and uses nutrition tables to calculate calories, protein, fat and carbs. You get a breakdown by ingredient that you can check and correct.

Where estimates go wrong most often

  • Oil, butter and sauces. A tablespoon of oil is about 90 kcal and is almost invisible in a photo.
  • Portion size. Without something to compare with, a large plate and a small one look alike.
  • Mixed dishes. Stews, casseroles, soups and dressed salads hide their ingredients.
  • Drinks. A glass of juice or a latte can weigh in like a whole snack.

How to take a better photo

  1. Show the whole plate, from above or at about 45°.
  2. Use good light without harsh shadows.
  3. A fork or spoon next to the plate helps judge the size.
  4. Add what can't be seen: “fried in oil”, “with mayonnaise”, “2 teaspoons of sugar”.
  5. If you know the weight (from the pack or scales), enter it — a real number always beats an estimate.
The key rule: after the photo, look through the ingredients and grams and fix anything that's off. A minute of checking makes your diary far more reliable.

Do you need perfect accuracy?

For most goals, no. Consistency matters more: if you log food every day the same way, you see the trend and can adjust your target by how your weight changes. The error in a single dish evens out over a week.

If you need high accuracy for medical reasons (for example carbohydrate counting for diabetes on insulin), use scales and label data, and treat photo estimates only as a helper.
