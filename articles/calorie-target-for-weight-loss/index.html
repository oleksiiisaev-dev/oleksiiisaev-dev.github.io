How many calories a day to lose weight: work out your own target

Vertex One · Updated 2026-11-18

To lose weight, your body has to use a little more energy than it gets from food. The hard part is knowing how much to eat. Too little brings hunger, tiredness and binges; too much and the scale doesn't move. Here is how to find your starting point step by step.

Step 1. Resting energy

Resting energy is what your body burns at rest: breathing, heart, brain and body heat. It is most often estimated with the Mifflin–St Jeor equation:

  • Men: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5
  • Women: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

Example: a woman aged 35, 165 cm, 75 kg. 750 + 1031 − 175 − 161 ≈ 1,445 kcal a day at complete rest.

Step 2. Add your activity

Multiply resting energy by an activity factor:

  • 1.2 — desk job, little movement
  • 1.375 — light activity, walks, 1–3 workouts a week
  • 1.55 — moderate activity, 3–5 workouts a week
  • 1.725 — very active, daily training or physical work

For our example with light activity: 1,445 × 1.375 ≈ 1,990 kcal. That is roughly the amount that keeps weight stable.

Step 3. A moderate deficit

For gradual weight loss, people usually subtract 300–500 kcal from that maintenance number. On average that is about 0.3–0.5 kg a week. In the example, about 1,500–1,700 kcal a day.

Don't stay below your resting energy for long, and don't go below about 1,200 kcal for women or 1,500 kcal for men without medical supervision. A very large deficit more often ends in binges, muscle loss and the weight coming back.

Why the scale jumps even when you do everything right

Body weight can change by 0.5–1.5 kg in a day because of water, salt, digestion and the hormonal cycle. Look at the weekly average, not a single weigh-in. If the average hasn't dropped in 2–3 weeks, lower the target by another 100–150 kcal or move a bit more.

What to eat within your target

  • Enough protein: it keeps you full longer and helps preserve muscle.
  • Vegetables, fruit, legumes and whole grains give volume and fibre for modest calories.
  • Less added sugar, sweet drinks and alcohol — “liquid” calories that hardly fill you up.

When to talk to a doctor first

If you have diabetes (especially on insulin or sulfonylureas), heart or kidney disease, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have had an eating disorder, discuss the goal and pace of weight loss with your doctor.
