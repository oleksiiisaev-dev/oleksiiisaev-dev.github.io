Sports nutrition: protein, BCAA, creatine and vitamins — when and why

Vertex One · Updated 2026-11-18

Sports supplements aren't magic powders. They are a convenient way to fill gaps in your diet. Here's what has good evidence behind it, how and when to take it, and what you can skip.

Food, sleep and training come first

No supplement replaces enough calories and protein from food, regular training and 7–9 hours of sleep. Supplements give you the last few percent of the result, not the first.

Protein: how much you need

People who train regularly are usually advised to get 1.4–2 g of protein per kg of body weight a day, food included. For a 70 kg person that's about 100–140 g. Above roughly 1.6 g/kg, the extra benefit for muscle is small on average.

  • Whey protein is absorbed quickly — handy after training or as a snack.
  • Casein is absorbed slowly — good before sleep or when you'll go a long time without food.
  • Plant protein (pea, rice, soy) suits vegetarians and people who don't tolerate milk. A pea and rice blend gives a fuller set of amino acids.

What matters most is your total protein for the day. Split it into 3–5 servings of 20–40 g. The “anabolic window” after training is much wider than once thought: you don't need to drink your shake the minute you finish.

BCAA: do you need them?

BCAA are only three of the nine essential amino acids. Building muscle takes all of them, so if you already eat enough protein, BCAA add almost nothing. A complete protein or ordinary protein-rich food gives you more for the same money. They may make sense during fasted training: 5–10 g.

Creatine — the best-studied supplement

Creatine monohydrate adds strength and power in short, intense efforts and helps you recover between sets. Take 3–5 g every day at any time, ideally with food. “Loading” (20 g a day for 5–7 days) works faster but isn't necessary.

In the first weeks your weight may go up by 1–2 kg because of water in the muscles — that isn't fat. In healthy people creatine at these doses is safe; with kidney disease, take it only after talking to your doctor.

Caffeine

Caffeine improves endurance and focus. The effective dose is 3–6 mg per kg of body weight 30–60 minutes before training; start at the lower end. Adults shouldn't exceed 400 mg a day from all sources including coffee, and pregnant women 200 mg. Don't take it within 6 hours of sleep.

Carbs during long workouts

Gels and sports drinks are for efforts longer than 60–90 minutes: about 30–60 g of carbs per hour, with water. For a usual one-hour gym session, water is enough. If you sweat a lot or it's hot, electrolytes help.

Vitamins and minerals

  • Vitamin D is often low in winter and with little time in the sun. Usually 1000–2000 IU a day with a meal containing fat; the dose is best chosen after a blood test. Don't go above 4000 IU a day without a doctor.
  • Omega-3 if you eat oily fish less than twice a week: about 1 g of EPA+DHA a day.
  • Magnesium: 200–400 mg a day, ideally in the evening; large doses can loosen stools.
  • Iron only after a blood test. Shortage is more common in women and vegetarians, but too much is harmful.
  • Multivitamins are a safety net for a limited or very low-calorie diet. Don't stack several products.
Choose supplements with independent quality testing (such as Informed Sport or NSF Certified for Sport), especially if you compete: supplements sometimes contain substances that aren't on the label, including banned ones.

Who should ask a doctor first

If you have kidney, liver or heart disease, high blood pressure or diabetes, are pregnant or breastfeeding, are under 18, or take regular medication (for example blood thinners), discuss any supplement with your doctor.
