How to read cosmetics ingredients: INCI, fragrance and allergens

Vertex One · Updated 2026-11-18

Cosmetic ingredients are listed by their international INCI names, mostly in Latin and English. At first it looks like a code, but a few rules make it easy to find your way.

Ingredient order

As with food, ingredients are listed in descending order of concentration. With one exception: anything below 1% may be listed in any order at the end. So “aloe extract” near the end may mean a very small amount.

Water (Aqua) usually comes first. Colours are shown as CI codes with five digits, usually at the end.

Fragrance and allergens

The word Parfum (or Fragrance, Aroma) can hide dozens of substances. But in the EU certain fragrance allergens (such as Limonene, Linalool, Citronellol and Geraniol) must be named separately when they exceed 0.001% in leave-on products and 0.01% in rinse-off products.

If your skin is sensitive or you've reacted to cosmetics before, choose fragrance-free products and do a patch test on a small area, such as the inside of the elbow, before first use.

Preservatives

Preservatives stop bacteria and fungi growing in products that contain water. They're allowed in limited concentrations. Some (such as methylisothiazolinone) cause allergic contact dermatitis more often, so the EU has restricted their use.

What the claims on the pack mean

  • “Hypoallergenic” doesn't guarantee there will be no reaction. There's no single strict definition.
  • “Natural” doesn't guarantee safety either: natural essential oils can be strong allergens.
  • “Paraben-free” only means one group of preservatives is absent.

When to see a doctor

Redness, itching, swelling or a rash after a cosmetic is a reason to stop using it. If the reaction is strong or keeps coming back, see a dermatologist or allergist: a patch test can show exactly what you react to.
